# Peter Lynch's Investment Principles

Distilled from *One Up on Wall Street* (1989), *Beating the Street* (1993), and Fidelity Magellan Fund annual letters (1977–1990). Lynch compounded at 29.2% annually over 13 years.

---

## 1. Invest in What You Know

> "The person that turns over the most rocks wins the game."

**The core insight:**
Lynch believed the ordinary investor has a significant edge over professional fund managers — they observe businesses as consumers, employees, and community members before Wall Street discovers them.

**How to apply it:**
- Pay attention to products/services you, your family, or colleagues adopt enthusiastically
- A store/restaurant concept you find compelling deserves a look at the balance sheet
- Industry insiders — doctors evaluating pharma, engineers evaluating capital goods — have informational advantages

**NSE application:**
- A consumer who notices Asian Paints' dominance in home improvement has a legitimate early observation
- A factory worker noticing a new machine brand everywhere in their plant has useful data

**The caveat:**
Observation is the starting point, not the conclusion. You still need to check whether the business has growth runway, sensible management, and reasonable valuation. Knowing a product is popular is not the same as knowing the stock is a buy.

## 2. The Six Stock Categories

Lynch classified every stock into one of six categories. The expected return, holding strategy, and monitoring frequency differ for each.

### 1. Slow Growers (Sluggards)
- Revenue/earnings growth: 2–5% per year
- Characteristics: large, mature companies; usually pay dividends; limited reinvestment opportunities
- Why own: dividend income and portfolio ballast
- Indian examples: Coal India, established PSU utilities
- Risk: Dividend cuts; market share loss to newer competitors

### 2. Stalwarts (Medium Growers)
- Revenue/earnings growth: 8–12% per year
- Characteristics: large, well-managed companies; unlikely to go bankrupt; unlikely to be ten-baggers
- Strategy: Buy below fair value, sell when P/E reaches historical high; rotate
- Indian examples: HUL, Nestle India, ITC, Asian Paints
- Risk: Overpaying at market peaks

### 3. Fast Growers
- Revenue/earnings growth: 20–30%+ per year
- Characteristics: small-to-mid cap; expanding concept; addressable market still large
- Strategy: Find early; monitor growth drivers carefully; have an exit plan for when growth saturates
- Indian examples: Dmart (in early years), Dixon Technologies, Polycab
- Risk: Overvaluation; market niche is smaller than assumed; execution failure
- Lynch's key question: *Is the concept still expanding or saturating?*

### 4. Cyclicals
- Revenue/earnings growth: volatile; highly correlated with economic cycle
- Characteristics: commodity producers, auto OEMs, steel, chemicals
- Strategy: Buy near cycle trough (earnings depressed, PE looks high); sell near cycle peak (earnings high, PE looks low)
- Critical insight: **A falling P/E on a cyclical is often a buy signal; a low P/E is often a sell signal**
- Indian examples: Tata Steel, Hindalco, ONGC, auto OEMs

### 5. Turnarounds
- Characteristics: companies in trouble that are recovering — could be industry-wide or company-specific
- Strategy: Only invest if the reason for decline was temporary, not structural; confirm the fix is underway
- Biggest upside potential but highest failure rate
- Indian examples: IDFC First Bank (transformation), Tata Motors (JLR recovery cycles)
- Lynch's test: *What will the company look like when it recovers?*

### 6. Asset Plays
- Characteristics: companies with hidden assets not reflected in the stock price
- Assets: real estate owned on old cost basis, cash/investments > market cap, demerger value, brand value
- Indian examples: Holding companies trading at deep discount to subsidiary value; companies with large treasury stock
- Key risk: The asset may never be unlocked for shareholders

## 3. The PEG Ratio — Lynch's Valuation Shorthand

> "The P/E ratio of any company that's fairly priced will equal its growth rate."

$$PEG = \frac{P/E}{\text{EPS Growth Rate (\%)}}$$

**Interpretation:**
- PEG < 1.0 → potentially undervalued for a growth company
- PEG = 1.0 → fairly valued
- PEG > 2.0 → expensive

**Lynch's refinement:**
- Dividend-paying companies: use `(EPS Growth + Dividend Yield) / P/E` — the "combined yield"
- Growth rate should be the **expected next 3–5 year EPS CAGR**, not trailing

## 4. The Ten-Bagger Mindset

Lynch coined the term "ten-bagger" — a stock that returns 10x (from baseball's double, triple, etc.).

**Where ten-baggers come from:**
- Small companies with large addressable markets
- Companies expanding geographically or into adjacent categories
- Businesses whose core concept is being copied/replicated successfully
- Turnarounds of companies with strong underlying assets

**Lynch's ten-bagger checklist:**
1. The story is still simple and easy to explain
2. Institutional ownership is low (under-followed = undiscovered)
3. The expansion is early — far more runway than what has already been executed
4. Management owns shares and is reinvesting intelligently
5. Margins are improving as scale increases
6. Valuation is reasonable relative to growth (PEG < 1.5)

---

## 5. The "Two-Minute Drill"

Lynch required every holding to pass a verbal test: explain in two minutes why you own the stock and what needs to happen for the investment thesis to play out.

**Good story:** "This company sells [X], which is growing because [Y]. It earns high returns on capital, has little debt, and the stock trades at a PEG of 0.8."

**Bad story:** "The chart looks good" / "My friend recommended it" / "The sector is hot"

This is a powerful anti-confirmation-bias tool — if you cannot explain the thesis clearly, you do not understand it.

---

## 6. Institutional Ownership — The Contrarian Edge

Lynch's key insight: **the best returns often come from stocks that institutions have not yet discovered.**

- If 60%+ of a stock is held by institutions and 30+ analysts cover it, any positive surprise is already priced in
- If a stock has <10% institutional ownership and 2–3 analysts, a small positive development can drive a large re-rating
- Boring names in boring industries tend to be under-owned — and therefore present more opportunity

---

## 7. Things to Avoid — Lynch's Warning List

1. **The hottest stock in the hottest sector** — everyone is already there; you are buying someone else's exit
2. **Diversifications (diworseification)** — companies straying far from their core competency through unrelated acquisitions
3. **Whisper stocks** — story stocks with no earnings, no revenue, just a "revolutionary" narrative
4. **Companies that depend on a single customer** — one lost contract can be devastating
5. **Companies named with an X or a Z** — (proxy for trendy/speculative naming; the business often has no real moat)
6. **The "next" something** — "the next Amazon," "the next HDFC Bank" — they rarely are

---

## 8. On Selling

Lynch was known for holding winners for long periods, but he had clear sell triggers:

**Sell a fast grower when:**
- The expansion story is fully played out (penetration in target markets is high)
- P/E has expanded far beyond the growth rate (PEG > 3x without acceleration in growth)
- Margins are compressing despite revenue growth
- Management is distracted by unrelated acquisitions

**Sell a stalwart when:**
- It has re-rated to the top of its historical P/E band
- Rotate into a more attractively valued name

**Never sell because:**
- The stock has gone up 50% (if the thesis is intact, the next 50% may be coming)
- The macro looks bad (Lynch was famously dismissive of macro forecasting)

---

## 9. Portfolio Monitoring — Know the Story, Not the Price

Lynch checked his companies' fundamentals semi-annually — not their stock prices daily.

The right questions to ask every 6 months:
1. Is the core growth thesis still intact?
2. Is the expansion still in early innings, or is it maturing?
3. Has management done anything to undermine trust?
4. Has valuation become stretched relative to growth?
5. Is there a better allocation opportunity available?

Price is only interesting in the context of value. Checking price without checking business progress is speculation.
