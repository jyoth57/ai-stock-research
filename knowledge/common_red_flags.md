# Common Red Flags in Stock Analysis

Red flags do not automatically mean a stock should be avoided. They are signals that require explanation. A company with one red flag and a good explanation is very different from a company with five red flags and evasive management.

---

## 1. Accounting Red Flags

### 1.1 Cash Flow Divergence
**The flag:** Net income growing consistently while operating cash flow is flat or falling.

Net income is an opinion; cash flow is a fact. When earnings and cash diverge for 2+ years, earnings quality is suspect.

**Check:** Operating Cash Flow / Net Income. Healthy range: 0.9–1.3x over a 5-year average.

- < 0.7x consistently → earnings contain significant non-cash items or aggressive accruals
- Negative OCF with positive Net Income → the company is not actually earning cash

---

### 1.2 Revenue Recognition Aggression
**The flag:** Revenue is recognised before cash is received, goods are delivered, or services are rendered.

**Warning signs:**
- Receivable days rising faster than revenue growth
- Deferred revenue declining rapidly (pulling future revenue forward)
- Bill-and-hold arrangements or milestone-based contracts with vague criteria
- Revenue restatements in prior periods

---

### 1.3 Inventory Build-Up
**The flag:** Inventory growing faster than revenue, or inventory days expanding.

**Possible explanations:**
- Demand slowdown (unsold goods piling up) — negative signal
- Deliberate stocking ahead of price increase — temporarily acceptable
- Accounting inflation of inventory to boost profits (margin of safety: physically verify if possible)

**Check:** Gross margin expanding while inventory days expanding is a red flag — suggests channel stuffing or overvaluing inventory.

---

### 1.4 Related Party Transactions
**The flag:** Significant transactions between the company and entities controlled by promoters/directors.

**Acceptable if:**
- Disclosed clearly in the annual report
- Conducted at arm's length (independently verified)
- Not material in scale (< 5% of revenue)

**Red flag if:**
- RPTs are rising as a % of revenue year after year
- Transactions involve unusual services, IP licensing, or management fees to promoter-owned entities
- Independent directors have not explicitly confirmed arm's-length basis
- Funds flow *to* promoter entities rather than *from* them (asset stripping)

---

### 1.5 Frequent Auditor Changes
**The flag:** Company changes its statutory auditor without a clear, benign reason.

Big 4 or top-10 Indian audit firm resignations are serious signals — auditors see the books and leave when they find problems they cannot sign off on.

**Also watch:** Auditor qualifications, emphasis-of-matter paragraphs, and going-concern notes in audit reports.

---

### 1.6 Capitalisation of Operating Expenses
**The flag:** Company capitalises expenses that peers expense immediately (e.g., marketing as "brand building," R&D as an intangible asset).

This inflates current earnings but inflates asset values and deferred amortisation. Compare capex/revenue ratios to sector peers — an outlier suggests creative accounting.

---

### 1.7 Goodwill and Impairment Avoidance
**The flag:** Large goodwill from acquisitions with no impairment, ever, regardless of acquired business performance.

Indian GAAP and Ind-AS require impairment testing. Companies that never impair goodwill despite acquired businesses underperforming are delaying inevitable write-downs that will crater reported earnings.

---

## 2. Balance Sheet Red Flags

### 2.1 Rising Promoter Pledge
**The flag:** Promoters have pledged their shareholding as collateral for personal or group company loans.

**Why it matters:**
- Pledged shares are sold by lenders if the stock price falls — triggering a spiral
- Promoter is financially stressed — may prioritise personal debt over business investment
- Pledged companies often have governance issues alongside the financial stress

**Threshold:** > 30% of promoter holding pledged = serious concern. Rising pledge trend is worse than absolute level.

---

### 2.2 Debt Hidden in Subsidiaries
**The flag:** Consolidated Debt/Equity is much higher than standalone.

Many Indian conglomerates use subsidiaries and SPVs to raise debt off the parent balance sheet. Always analyse the consolidated statements.

**Check:** Compare standalone vs. consolidated D/E, interest coverage, and cash balances.

---

### 2.3 Contingent Liabilities Exceeding Net Worth
**The flag:** Guarantees, disputed taxes, litigation provisions, or off-balance-sheet obligations that could crystallise.

The notes to financial statements contain contingent liability disclosures — they are not on the face of the balance sheet.

**Threshold:** Total contingent liabilities > 50% of net worth warrants deep investigation.

---

### 2.4 Deteriorating Working Capital
**The flag:** Debtor days rising + Creditor days falling + Inventory days rising = cash being consumed by working capital.

This often precedes a cash crunch. Companies that need to discount heavily to collect receivables may have a product acceptance problem.

---

### 2.5 Declining Cash Despite Positive Earnings
**The flag:** The company reports profits but its cash balance keeps declining.

Possible causes:
- Cash is being consumed by working capital expansion
- Capex is exceeding depreciation and FCF
- Dividends/buybacks are funded by new debt
- Cash is being transferred to related entities

---

## 3. Management and Governance Red Flags

### 3.1 Promoter Compensation Out of Proportion
**The flag:** Promoter/Director remuneration is a large and rising % of PAT.

**Check:** Total managerial remuneration / PAT. > 10% in a profitable company is a yellow flag. > 20% is a red flag.

Indian Companies Act caps promoter remuneration at 5–11% of net profits, but creative structuring (consultancy fees, rent to promoter-owned entities) can circumvent this.

---

### 3.2 Frequent Change of Key Management
**The flag:** CFO, CEO, or statutory auditor turnover is higher than industry norms.

- Multiple CFO changes in 3 years is a serious warning — CFOs resign when they cannot sign off on accounts
- Independent director resignations with no stated reason should trigger immediate scrutiny

---

### 3.3 Vague or Evasive Investor Communication
**The flag:** Management avoids quantifying guidance, gives circular answers to specific questions, and changes the narrative every quarter.

**Best practice benchmark:** High-quality managements give honest earnings guidance, explain shortfalls clearly, and maintain consistent accounting policies year over year.

**Test:** Compare MD&A language year-over-year. Does management acknowledge failures explicitly? Or reframe poor outcomes as "investments for the future"?

---

### 3.4 Promoter Selling While Company Is Buying Back
**The flag:** Company announces share buyback but promoters are simultaneously selling in the secondary market.

This is the opposite of alignment — promoters are offloading to the company treasury at the expense of public shareholders.

---

### 3.5 Track Record of Failed Acquisitions
**The flag:** Multiple large acquisitions at high prices, followed by write-downs.

Each failed acquisition is a capital allocation failure. A management team with two or more such failures should be assessed at a significant discount to peers.

---

## 4. Business and Competitive Red Flags

### 4.1 Customer Concentration Risk
**The flag:** > 20% of revenue from a single customer.

Loss of one customer can permanently impair earnings. Check: are the contracts long-term and sticky, or transactional?

---

### 4.2 Margin Compression With No Explanation
**The flag:** EBITDA margins declining for 3+ consecutive quarters without a clear cyclical or input-cost reason.

Structural margin compression usually indicates: pricing power erosion, increased competition, or mix shift to lower-margin products. These are often permanent, not temporary.

---

### 4.3 Revenue Growth Without Profitability
**The flag:** Company growing revenue 20–30% YoY but losses are widening, not narrowing.

A business model that doesn't approach profitability as it scales has a unit economics problem. Ask: *At what scale does this business become profitable? How are they funding losses?*

---

### 4.4 Capital-Intensive Business with Low Returns
**The flag:** High asset base (D/A > 0.7x), high capex/revenue (> 15%), and ROCE below cost of capital (< 10%).

These companies destroy value with every rupee invested. They only look like they are "growing" — they are actually burning capital.

---

### 4.5 The "Story Stock" with No Numbers
**The flag:** The investment case is entirely based on a narrative ("AI will transform their business," "huge land bank," "upcoming product launch") with no current financial support.

Narrative without numbers is speculation. The burden of proof is on the bull case: *when does the story produce earnings, and how confident are we?*

---

## 5. Red Flags Ranked by Severity

| Severity | Red Flag |
|---|---|
| **Critical** | Promoter pledge > 50% and rising |
| **Critical** | Auditor resignation or qualification |
| **Critical** | Negative OCF for 3+ years with positive net income |
| **Critical** | SEBI action / regulatory investigation |
| **High** | RPTs > 15% of revenue and growing |
| **High** | CFO changed twice in 3 years |
| **High** | Goodwill > 50% of net worth with zero impairment ever |
| **Medium** | Receivable days rising > 30 days YoY |
| **Medium** | Promoter remuneration > 15% of PAT |
| **Medium** | Customer concentration > 25% in one client |
| **Low** | Guidance missed once with clear explanation |
| **Low** | Single-year margin compression with identified input cost |
