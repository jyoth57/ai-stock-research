# Investment Principles

## 1. The Primacy of Business Quality

A great business at a fair price beats a fair business at a great price every time over the long run.

**What makes a business high quality?**
- Generates returns on capital consistently above its cost of capital (ROCE > 15% sustained)
- Earns pricing power — can raise prices without losing volume
- Operates in a growing industry or holds a dominant position in a stable one
- Requires minimal reinvestment to maintain earnings (high free cash flow conversion)
- Produces recurring or predictable revenue streams

---

## 2. The Four Sources of Competitive Advantage (Moat)

| Moat Type | Description | Indian Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost advantage** | Structurally lower costs than peers | Coal India, ONGC |
| **Switching costs** | Customer pain of leaving is high | TCS, Infosys (enterprise IT), HDFC Bank (salary accounts) |
| **Network effects** | Product/service improves as more users join | NSE, BSE, CDSL |
| **Intangibles** | Brand, patents, regulatory licences | Asian Paints, Pidilite, Page Industries |

A moat without reinvestment runway is a melting iceberg. Check whether the moat is widening or narrowing.

---

## 3. Capital Allocation — The Most Underrated Skill

Management that allocates capital well compounds wealth; poor allocators destroy it at scale.

Hierarchy of capital deployment (best to worst for minority shareholders):
1. Reinvest at high ROIC into core business
2. Bolt-on acquisitions at sensible prices
3. Debt repayment when leverage is high
4. Share buybacks when stock trades below intrinsic value
5. Dividends (signals lack of reinvestment opportunity)
6. Large unrelated diversification — usually destroys value

**Red signal:** Companies that perpetually raise equity capital while paying dividends are destroying value for existing shareholders.

---

## 4. The Balance Sheet as a Safety Net

Earnings can be manufactured; cash cannot be faked indefinitely.

- Prefer net-cash or low-net-debt companies (Net Debt/EBITDA < 2x)
- Working capital discipline matters: rising debtor days + falling inventory turns = trouble
- Contingent liabilities, pledged promoter shares, and off-balance-sheet obligations are hidden bombs
- A debt-free company buying growth is far safer than a leveraged company surviving on refinancing

---

## 5. Management Quality Framework

Assess on three axes:

**Competence**
- Track record of execution vs. stated targets
- Capital allocation history (acquisitions, capex returns)
- Ability to navigate downturns without permanent damage

**Integrity**
- Related-party transactions at arm's length?
- Promoter compensation proportional to performance?
- Consistent accounting policies across years?
- Honest about failures in shareholder communications?

**Ownership alignment**
- Promoter skin in the game (holding > 40% is generally positive)
- ESOPs designed to reward long-term creation, not short-term price
- Promoter pledge trend: rising pledge = distress signal

---

## 6. Growth: Distinguishing Quality from Quantity

Not all growth is equal.

| Growth type | Characteristics | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Organic + profitable | Revenue grows, margins stable/expanding | Best |
| Acquisition-driven | ROIC of acquired assets > WACC | Acceptable if disciplined |
| Commodity-price driven | Revenue up but margins volatile | Caution |
| Leveraged growth | Debt-fuelled expansion at thin spreads | Dangerous |
| Equity-dilutive | EPS flat despite revenue growth | Value-destructive |

Ask: **Is EPS growing at least as fast as revenue?** If not, the growth is leaking to someone else.

---

## 7. Valuation as Risk Management, Not Target-Setting

Valuation does not tell you what a stock will do next quarter. It tells you the margin of safety you have against being wrong.

- **Never pay for perfection.** A perfect business at a perfect price has no room for error.
- Paying 60x earnings for a 20% grower requires that growth to sustain for 7+ years just to break even.
- Valuation matters most at extremes — wildly cheap or wildly expensive; in the middle, focus on quality.
- Use valuation bands (5-year P/E range, EV/EBITDA vs. sector) rather than point estimates.

---

## 8. Portfolio Construction Principles

- **Concentration over diversification** for high-conviction ideas (15–25 stocks is sufficient)
- Do not hold a stock you cannot explain in two sentences
- Position sizing should reflect both conviction AND liquidity
- Rebalance by trimming winners that have become too large, not by selling losers
- Keep 5–10% cash as an option, not as a goal

---

## 9. Behaviour Matters More Than Intelligence

The investor's chief problem — and even his worst enemy — is likely to be himself. — Benjamin Graham

- Buy when others are fearful; scrutinise when others are euphoric
- A falling stock is not automatically cheap; a rising stock is not automatically expensive
- Do not confuse activity with progress — fewer trades, better decisions
- Write down your investment thesis before buying; review it when selling

---

## 10. Circle of Competence

Only invest in businesses you genuinely understand — their economics, their competitive drivers, and what could go wrong.

**Expanding your circle:**
- Read annual reports, not just quarterly results
- Study the whole industry, not just one company
- Talk to customers, suppliers, and ex-employees
- Follow the sector for at least one full cycle before investing heavily
