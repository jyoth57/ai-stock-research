# Warren Buffett's Investment Principles

Distilled from Berkshire Hathaway annual letters (1965–present), shareholder meetings, and authorised biographies.

---

## 1. Circle of Competence

> "I don't look to jump over 7-foot bars; I look around for 1-foot bars that I can step over."

**The principle:**
- Only invest in businesses you can genuinely understand — their economics, competitive position, and what could go wrong
- The size of your circle is less important than knowing its boundaries
- Saying "I don't know" is a strength, not a weakness

**What it means in practice:**
- Read 10 years of annual reports before investing in an unfamiliar business
- If you cannot explain the business model to a 12-year-old, you don't understand it well enough
- Technology companies that Buffett avoided in the 1990s were outside his circle — not stupid, just disciplined
- Exception: when the evidence is overwhelming (e.g., Apple's consumer lock-in), expand the circle deliberately

**Application for NSE stocks:**
- Build deep expertise in 3–4 sectors before diversifying into others
- Avoid complex financial structures (hybrid instruments, SPVs, subsidiaries without full disclosure) unless you are a genuine expert

## 2. Economic Moat — The Castle Analogy

> "The key to investing is not assessing how much an industry is going to affect society, or how much it will grow, but rather determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage."

**Types Buffett prizes most:**
1. **Consumer brands with pricing power** (Coca-Cola, See's Candies / Indian equivalents: Asian Paints, Pidilite, Nestle India)
2. **Low-cost producer in a commodity** (GEICO's cost advantage in insurance)
3. **Network effects** (American Express / Indian: CDSL, NSE)
4. **Switching costs** (Wells Fargo's banking relationships)

**The durability test:**
- Could a competitor replicate this advantage with unlimited capital? If yes, it is not a moat.
- Has the moat held through at least one recession? If not, it is unproven.
- Is the moat **widening** (increasing returns at the margin) or **narrowing** (competition eroding share)?

**The toll bridge metaphor:** Buffett likes businesses that are the only bridge across a river — they can raise tolls without losing traffic.

## 3. Management Quality — Honest, Able, and Aligned

> "We look for three things: integrity, intelligence, and energy. And if they don't have the first, the other two will kill you."

**Integrity checklist:**
- Does the CEO write honestly about failures in annual letters, or only celebrate successes?
- Is compensation sensible relative to the industry and company performance?
- Have they ever misled shareholders — even once? (Buffett's rule: one dishonest act disqualifies permanently)
- Do they allocate capital to shareholders when reinvestment opportunities are poor? Or do they empire-build?

**Ability checklist:**
- Is the business better positioned competitively than when this management took over?
- Have returns on capital held or improved under their tenure?
- Do they make the difficult decisions (plant closures, exiting bad businesses)?

**Alignment checklist:**
- Does management own significant stock? (Not options — actual shares bought with own money)
- Is their compensation linked to per-share value, or to revenue/size metrics?

## 4. Financial Characteristics Buffett Seeks

### Earnings quality:
- High and consistent return on equity **without excessive leverage** (>15% ROE with D/E < 0.5x is ideal)
- Owner earnings = Net Income + Depreciation − Maintenance Capex (this is the true cash generation)
- Avoid businesses that require constant reinvestment just to maintain their position

### The "one-dollar test":
> "For each dollar retained by a company, at least one dollar of market value should be created."

If a company has retained ₹100 of earnings over 5 years but market cap has grown by less than ₹100, management has been a poor capital allocator.

### Balance sheet discipline:
- Little or no long-term debt (exception: regulated utilities where debt is structural)
- Avoid companies with complex capital structures — they are usually hiding something
- Large cash reserves are acceptable if management has proven capital allocation skills

## 5. Price vs. Value — Margin of Safety

> "Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."

**Key concepts:**
- Intrinsic value is the discounted value of all future owner earnings
- Never pay a price that assumes everything goes right — businesses face unexpected headwinds
- **Margin of safety:** Buy at a significant discount to intrinsic value so that even if you are somewhat wrong, you do not lose money

**The two-part equation:**
1. Is this a wonderful business?
2. Am I buying it at a price that gives me a margin of safety?

Both must be true. A wonderful business bought at a terrible price can be a mediocre investment for years.

## 6. The Long Term — "Our Favourite Holding Period is Forever"

> "If you aren't willing to own a stock for 10 years, don't even think about owning it for 10 minutes."

**Why long holding periods win:**
- Compounding is asymmetric — each year of above-market returns builds on the prior base
- Transaction costs, taxes, and behavioural errors accumulate for frequent traders
- Businesses with strong moats improve their competitive positions over decades

**When to sell:**
1. When the business has **permanently** deteriorated (not temporarily)
2. When management integrity is compromised
3. When an obviously better opportunity exists and reallocation improves returns materially
4. When the original investment thesis has been **proven wrong** — not just when the price falls

Do NOT sell because: the stock doubled, you "locked in profits," macro looks uncertain, or you made a prediction on a news event.

## 7. On Diversification

> "Diversification is protection against ignorance. It makes little sense if you know what you are doing."

- For someone who does their own research deeply: 8–15 stocks is sufficient
- Wide diversification is a hedge against not knowing what you own
- Concentration in high-conviction ideas is how outsized returns are generated

**Buffett's personal rule:** He would be comfortable putting 10% of net worth in his top idea if it were available at the right price. The question is never "should I diversify?" but "do I know this business well enough to concentrate?"

---

## 8. Macro and Market Predictions

> "We have long felt that the only value of stock forecasters is to make fortune tellers look good."

- Never make investment decisions based on macroeconomic or market forecasts
- The economy will always have problems; good businesses survive them
- Fear and greed create opportunities — **be greedy when others are fearful, fearful when others are greedy**
- Never try to predict the direction of interest rates, currencies, or the market

---

## 9. On Mr. Market

From Benjamin Graham: The market is a manic-depressive business partner — he offers to buy or sell his shares every day at wildly varying prices. You are under no obligation to transact.

**Buffett's application:**
- Use market volatility as a tool — buy when Mr. Market is depressed and selling at irrational prices
- Ignore Mr. Market when he is euphoric
- Short-term price movements are noise; focus on business value

---

## 10. The Newspaper Test

Before any investment or business decision, ask:

> "Would I be comfortable if this decision appeared on the front page of tomorrow's newspaper, written by a reporter who covers both corporate malfeasance AND overly conservative/missed-opportunity decisions?"

This dual test guards against both ethical failures and excessive timidity.
